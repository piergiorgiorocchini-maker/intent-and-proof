| Privacy review | Check forms, tracking, data sharing, storage, consent and processor roles |
| Approval | The practice and the appropriately authorised person approve the final version |
| Audit trail | Preserve versions, evidence, approvals and the date of review |

The infrastructure can provide market-specific templates, a claims register, consent records, approval status, role-based access, change history and data-minimisation rules. It cannot replace qualified local advice or transfer the final responsibility away from the licensed provider.

This distinction also protects the commercial promise. Intent & Proof should not claim “global healthcare compliance”, an impressive phrase that collapses the moment two legal systems disagree. The defensible promise is more precise:

> **The system is designed to support jurisdiction-aware healthcare communication through controlled templates, documented approval workflows and separation between clinical and marketing data. Final approval remains with the licensed provider and the qualified advisers responsible in the relevant market.**

Compliance does not have to weaken the content. Informational pages that explain assessment, alternatives, evidence, limitations and the patient journey often provide better reasons to choose a practice than aggressive promotion. The constraint can become an editorial advantage when it is turned into clarity, proof and a more responsible decision process.

This section is informational and architectural. It is not legal advice. Every implementation must be checked against the current rules, regulators and professional obligations that apply to the practice's jurisdiction.

## The front desk is part of the acquisition system

An online enquiry does not become an appointment automatically because a form was submitted. Between submission and the first visit lies a human process shaped by timing, tone, availability, clarity and record-keeping.

Many practices treat front-desk work as separate from marketing. Campaigns deliver contacts; from that point, the outcome is attributed vaguely either to lead quality or to the receptionist's ability. That separation prevents the practice from seeing where the journey breaks.

The front desk should not “sell the treatment”. Its role is to take responsibility for the enquiry, understand the non-clinical context, explain the next step and facilitate an appropriate first visit.

### Contact rate before booking rate

Before asking how many leads book an appointment, the practice needs to know how many are actually reached. A form may contain an incorrect number, a person may not answer or the call may take place at an unsuitable time.

The CRM should record at least:

- first attempt;
- channel used;
- outcome;
- subsequent attempts;
- appointment booked or reason not booked;
- confirmation;
- attendance or no-show.

These stages distinguish a demand-quality problem from an intake problem.

### Response time as an internal service level

Marketing discussions often repeat a five-minute rule. Speed matters, but no universal threshold guarantees the same result for every practice and treatment.

It is more useful to define a realistic service level: for example, acknowledgement during opening hours within a specified window, an immediate confirmation message and scheduled follow-up attempts. The practice should measure the relationship between its own response time, contact rate and booking rate.

An impossible promise damages the experience. When the website says “we will call you immediately” and the front desk responds the next day, the problem begins before the telephone conversation.

### The script as guidance, not aggressive sales copy

A script can help staff maintain consistency, especially when enquiries concern complex treatments. It should not turn the conversation into telemarketing.

The structure may include:

1. confirmation of the enquiry and the practice's identity;
2. a question about the general reason for contact, without attempting inappropriate medical history-taking;
3. an explanation of the type of first visit;
4. clarification that advice and cost require assessment;
5. proposed availability;
6. confirmation of contact details and instructions;
7. recording the outcome.

Price objections should not be “overcome” with persuasion techniques. The front desk can explain that cost depends on assessment and that the visit is required to define options and a treatment plan. Staff should also know when the conversation needs to be transferred to a clinician.

### The value of the first visit

Many price-focused enquiries arise because the first visit is perceived as an administrative step. Communication should explain what the visit includes: gathering information, assessment, possible investigations, discussion of alternatives and definition of the next step.

The practice must avoid excessive promises. If the first visit does not include a complete diagnosis or final quotation, that should be stated. Clarity reduces misunderstanding and no-shows.

### Confirmations and reminders

An appointment booked several weeks after the enquiry can be forgotten or lose priority. Confirmations and reminders reduce uncertainty when they include useful information: date, location, time, any documents to bring, how to make changes and whom to contact.

Automation should leave space for human handling. A patient who replies to a reminder with a question should not enter a dead end.

### No-shows: a metric that must be segmented

The non-attendance rate should be analysed by source, campaign, treatment, delay between enquiry and appointment, and confirmation method. A high rate may indicate:

- poorly formed expectations;
- appointments scheduled too far in the future;
- irrelevant enquiries;
- weak follow-up;
- logistical difficulty;
- inadequate explanation of the visit's value.
